
## Part 1: State-of-the-Art Report (The Global AI Divide)

The geopolitical architecture of the global artificial intelligence (AI) ecosystem is fracturing into three distinct paradigms, fundamentally altering how economic surplus is generated, captured, and distributed across the AI value stack. The core inquiry of this analysis—whether the AI value stack possesses a single global shape or if different optima apply per geopolitical bloc—reveals that technological convergence is an illusion. Instead, the United States, the People's Republic of China, and the European Union are actively engineering structurally divergent value stacks dictated by their respective geopolitical imperatives, capital market structures, and regulatory philosophies.

Currently, the geopolitical dynamics of AI are fiercely reinforcing economic value concentration patterns within specific geographic and corporate loci, while simultaneously disrupting the traditional globalization of technology. In the United States, a techno-optimist, market-driven model has consolidated unprecedented capital and computing power within a tight oligopoly of hyperscalers and frontier model developers. Value here is highly concentrated at the compute and proprietary model layers, driven by a national security framing that views AI as a winner-takes-all sprint toward Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). Conversely, China, constrained by severe U.S. export controls, is leveraging a state-directed strategy that prioritizes the rapid commoditization of the model layer through open-weight architectures. By treating foundation models as open-source infrastructure, China is aggressively pushing value downstream toward application, enterprise integration, and systemic diffusion. Europe has opted for a rights-driven, regulatory model, attempting to exert influence through standard-setting and compliance frameworks rather than raw compute or model generation.

Four critical structural differences define this tripolar divide:

1. **The Locus of Value Extraction:** The U.S. seeks economic rent through closed-API frontier models and cloud computing dominance. China seeks economic surplus through highly efficient, industry-specific application deployment and the global export of open-weight ecosystems that undermine Western pricing power. Europe seeks to capture value through "sovereign AI" infrastructure and the global licensing of its compliance and trust frameworks.
    
2. **Innovation versus Diffusion Capacity:** The U.S. optimizes for breakthrough innovation (creating new-to-the-world technologies), whereas China is structurally shifting to optimize for diffusion—the ability to embed these innovations widely across traditional industrial and enterprise sectors to drive macro-economic productivity.
    
3. **Approaches to Model Accessibility:** The U.S. predominantly treats frontier weights as proprietary intellectual property and national security assets. China embraces open-weight models as asymmetric weapons to undercut Western licensing moats and establish Chinese architectures as the foundational operating systems of the Global South.
    
4. **Regulatory Posture and Global Expansion:** As outlined by Anu Bradford, each bloc exports a different form of power. The U.S. exports private corporate power; China exports digital infrastructure power; the EU exports regulatory power via the "Brussels Effect".
    

Regarding the European Union, current evidence strongly indicates that the regulatory framework—specifically the AI Act—is functioning as a severe handicap for European operators in the short-to-medium term, rather than a protective moat. Startups face staggering compliance bottlenecks, forcing them to divert scarce venture capital toward legal overhead rather than compute. Furthermore, the "Brussels Effect" faces structural limitations in the AI era; while Europe can export compliance paperwork, it struggles to export genuine fundamental rights protections to state-controlled domains globally. However, in the long term, if the global market for enterprise and government AI strictly demands verifiable safety, auditability, and fundamental rights protections, Europe's regulatory architecture could theoretically mature into an institutional moat, establishing the continent as the obligatory clearinghouse for trusted AI.

## Part 2: The United States: Hyperscaler Dominance and the Frontier Sprint

The United States currently dominates the global AI index, ranking first in absolute investment, innovation, and implementation. The American digital governance model is built upon a techno-libertarian, market-driven foundation that historically prioritized free markets, free speech, and rapid innovation over state-imposed guardrails. In the context of generative AI, this philosophy has facilitated the massive concentration of capital, talent, and compute resources within a highly integrated ecosystem of semiconductor designers and cloud hyperscalers.

### The Concentration of Capital and the Proprietary Moat

The American AI value stack is characterized by vertical integration and the hoarding of frontier capabilities. Helen Toner, a leading voice in AI governance, observes that AI is an inherently power-concentrating technology due to its extreme capital intensity. Because training state-of-the-art models requires billions of dollars in specialized hardware and energy infrastructure, economic surplus naturally pools at the top of the supply chain. Leading U.S. firms argue that the future of AGI is a "winner-take-all" scenario; consequently, keeping model weights closed and proprietary is viewed as an absolute economic necessity to recoup massive intellectual property investments.

This concentration is further entrenched by U.S. policy framing, which increasingly views AI development through the strict lens of great power competition and national security. The U.S. government's strategy—exemplified by aggressive semiconductor export controls aimed at throttling China's access to advanced chips—treats the AI race as a discrete sprint toward AGI. In this paradigm, national security circles operate under the assumption that the first nation to achieve a definitive AGI breakthrough will secure insurmountable economic and military dominance.

However, Toner critiques this approach, arguing that U.S. policy lacks a clear, consistent set of long-term goals, particularly regarding the efficacy of semiconductor export controls. She warns that focusing exclusively on the "non-proliferation" of AI models—attempting to prevent models from spreading—is a losing battle that would ultimately require authoritarian levels of domestic oversight. Instead, Toner advocates for a shift in policy focus toward "adaptation buffers" to manage societal misuse and emphasizes the "steerability" of AI systems rather than abstract alignment to a singular, poorly defined objective. By failing to build robust frameworks for managing AI risk beyond simple non-proliferation, the U.S. risks creating an ecosystem that is highly innovative but societally fragile.

### Innovation vs. Diffusion: The Illusion of the Sprint

While the U.S. leads in sheer innovation capacity, leading scholars warn that the strategic framing of the AI race as a short-term sprint is fundamentally flawed. Jeffrey Ding, a prominent researcher on U.S.-China technology competition, argues that national security circles are training for the wrong event. According to Ding's research on General Purpose Technologies (GPTs), historical economic power transitions are rarely decided by the initial invention of a breakthrough technology. Instead, they are determined by "diffusion capacity"—the ability of a nation to adapt its institutions to spread and adopt the technology broadly across all productive sectors.

Ding demonstrates this through historical analogs: during the Second Industrial Revolution, the U.S. surpassed competitors not merely by inventing new technologies, but by diffusing them rapidly across its vast industrial base. Conversely, in the early postwar period, the Soviet Union possessed immense innovation capacity—launching the first satellite and achieving major breakthroughs in physics—but suffered from a massive diffusion deficit, ultimately failing to translate scientific triumphs into broad economic productivity.

The U.S. currently possesses strong diffusion capabilities relative to its peers, largely due to a broad base of engineering skills and institutions capable of absorbing new technologies. However, by over-indexing on frontier breakthroughs (the "leading-sector mechanism") and prioritizing the restriction of foreign access over domestic integration, the U.S. risks neglecting its own diffusion mandate. Ding advises that Washington should focus on an "AI diffusion marathon"—widening the base of AI engineers, supporting public-private partnerships, and helping small and medium enterprises (SMEs) develop their AI capabilities. Failure to do so will result in a top-heavy economy where the economic surplus of AI is trapped within the technology sector itself, yielding high corporate valuations for hyperscalers but failing to drive broad-based industrial renewal.

### The Dynamics of Value Landing in the U.S.

In the U.S. model, value lands predominantly at the infrastructure and foundation model layers. Because the regulatory environment (historically shielded by frameworks like Section 230) allows tech companies to scale with minimal liability , U.S. firms have amassed tremendous economic, political, and cultural power, operating as "digital empires". The strategic moat is sheer scale—a capital and compute barrier that cannot be crossed by smaller actors. Consequently, the redistribution of economic surplus heavily favors the providers of compute (Nvidia) and the orchestrators of massive proprietary models (Microsoft/OpenAI, Google, AWS), who extract rent by metering access to intelligence via Application Programming Interfaces (APIs).

## Part 3: China: State-Directed Diffusion and the Open-Weight Asymmetry

China’s approach to AI value capture represents a stark structural divergence from the United States. While China consistently ranks second globally in AI capacity, vibrancy, and readiness indices , it operates under a state-driven model designed to leverage technology for systemic economic restructuring, state security, and infrastructural export. Facing intense exogenous pressures—most notably the U.S. embargo on high-end semiconductors—China has been forced to engineer an entirely different optimum for its AI value stack.

### Addressing the Diffusion Deficit

A central pillar of understanding China's AI ecosystem is Jeffrey Ding's concept of the "diffusion deficit." Debates about national scientific power frequently center on innovation capacity—the generation of novel advances. By these metrics, China appears to be an ascendant superpower, matching or exceeding the U.S. in patent generation and academic publications. However, Ding argues that there is often a substantial gap between a state's capacity to bring forth inventions and its capacity to adopt innovations at scale.

China historically struggles with this diffusion deficit due to uneven technical and STEM education across its broader population, prioritizing an innovation-centric approach that isolates breakthroughs within elite institutions. Acknowledging this vulnerability, the Chinese state and its corporate national champions are executing a strategic reorientation. Rather than competing solely at the frontier model layer—where U.S. compute advantages are overwhelming—China is aggressively pivoting toward application, system integration, and enterprise deployment.

### The Open-Weight Asymmetry and 01.AI

This strategic reorientation is vividly illustrated by the rise of open-weight models. By releasing highly capable models as open-source infrastructure, Chinese firms are systematically commoditizing the foundational layer where U.S. hyperscalers attempt to extract rent. Kai-Fu Lee, founder of the Beijing-based AI firm 01.AI, explicitly articulates this strategy. Recognizing that the vast majority of AI developers and global entities cannot afford to build super-platforms like OpenAI, 01.AI opted to build an open-source model ecosystem.

01.AI’s trajectory underscores the resilience of the Chinese ecosystem. Anticipating U.S. export controls, the company went into debt to stockpile graphics processing units (GPUs) before the embargo tightened. Operating with a constrained supply of chips, 01.AI developed highly efficient AI infrastructure and inference engines, maintaining a chip-cluster failure rate lower than the industry average. The result was the Yi-series of models, including the Yi-34B (specifically sized to run on affordable hardware) and the Yi-Lightning mixture-of-experts model.

Crucially, 01.AI has aggressively pivoted away from consumer applications (B2C) toward enterprise transformations (B2B). Lee notes that the fierce winner-take-all dynamics of the Chinese consumer market—where platforms must encompass all functions to survive as "super-apps"—make B2C AI applications highly volatile. Instead, 01.AI targets traditional industries such as banking, insurance, mining, and energy. This is a direct, calculated attempt to bridge China's diffusion deficit—embedding AI directly into the gears of the macroeconomy. As Lee states, it is essential to provide "white-glove service" to companies unprepared for digital transformation, co-creating applications that generate tangible business outcomes rather than mere cost savings.

### The DeepSeek "Sputnik Moment"

The U.S. strategy of export controls has inadvertently catalyzed a surge in Chinese algorithmic efficiency, creating a geopolitical blowback effect. The recent breakthrough by the Chinese startup DeepSeek represents what analysts and international media term an AI "Sputnik moment". Barred from accessing Nvidia's cutting-edge hardware, DeepSeek utilized less-advanced H800 chips—permitted for export until late 2023—and pioneered highly optimized toolchains and algorithmic architectures to match American frontier models at a fraction of the compute training cost.

DeepSeek’s release of its V4 and R1 models is viewed within China as "betting on the Nation's destiny". The models shattered the "Impossible Triangle" of high performance, low cost, and open-source accessibility. Furthermore, DeepSeek heavily optimized its deployment for Huawei's domestic Ascend chips, representing a historic breakthrough in "chip-model partnership" and advancing the critical state goal of domestic semiconductor substitution.

By providing these models freely, Chinese firms are establishing a de facto standard for the developing world. As Chinese commentators explicitly note, the goal is for Chinese architectures to become the "Android of the AI era"—an open ecosystem that developers globally build upon, effectively undermining the global pricing power of Western closed models.

### Exporting Infrastructure Power

Anu Bradford notes that while the U.S. exports private power, China exports "infrastructure power" via its Digital Silk Road. China blankets developing regions in Africa, Latin America, and Asia with 5G networks, undersea cables, data centers, and surveillance systems. These developing countries readily adopt Chinese infrastructure because it is affordable, effective, and fulfills immediate developmental and law enforcement needs, rendering concerns about data privacy secondary.

The commoditization of AI through open-weight models perfectly aligns with this strategy. Kai-Fu Lee emphasizes that for almost all countries outside the U.S. and China, the practical alternative for AI sovereignty is to take a leading open-source model and continue training it for the country's specific language, values, and regulations. By providing the foundational models for this process, China ensures that the global value stack relies on Chinese underlying frameworks, capturing geopolitical loyalty and economic integration from nations that prioritize rapid development over Western democratic norms.

## Part 4: Europe: Regulatory Imperialism, the Brussels Mirage, and Sovereign AI

The European Union represents the third pole in the global digital ecosystem, fundamentally defined by a "rights-driven" regulatory model. Viewing the Chinese model as too oppressive and the U.S. model as too permissive, the EU seeks to check corporate power, protect democratic structures, and ensure a fairer redistribution of digital gains. Lacking the native hyperscalers of the U.S. and the state-directed digital conglomerates of China, Europe seeks to shape the AI value stack exogenously through regulatory imperialism and natively through the pursuit of Sovereign AI.

### The AI Index and Structural Deficits

Empirical data reveals a significant performance gap separating the U.S. and China from the next tier of countries. According to Tortoise Media's Global AI Index, the U.S. and China dominate the landscape, followed by nations like Singapore, the United Kingdom, Canada, and South Korea. European nations populate the secondary tiers: Germany ranks 8th (strong in talent but lagging elsewhere), followed closely by Finland and the Netherlands. France ranks 11th, Denmark 14th, and Sweden 25th.

Anu Bradford argues that Europe's technological lag is not inherently caused by its regulations, but rather by deep structural and ecosystem deficits. Europe lacks a truly integrated digital single market; tech companies struggle to scale across 27 different markets due to persistent legal, cultural, and linguistic barriers. Furthermore, Europe lacks a capital markets union and possesses a venture capital ecosystem that is minuscule compared to the U.S., compounded by differing societal attitudes toward risk-taking and failure.

### The AI Act: Moat or Handicap?

To exert global influence, the EU relies on the "Brussels Effect"—the phenomenon whereby the EU leverages its massive consumer market to dictate global corporate behavior. By establishing stringent standards, the EU incentivizes non-European firms to standardize their global operations to EU compliance levels rather than managing fragmented regulatory regimes. This strategy has been fortified by the Digital Markets Act (DMA) and the Digital Services Act (DSA), which directly target the business models of large "gatekeeper" platforms.

With the passage of the sweeping EU AI Act, Europe aims to establish the definitive global standard for trustworthy AI. However, this regulatory posture carries immense risks. The AI Act has sparked severe backlash from Europe's startup and venture capital ecosystem. Dozens of leading European founders and VCs—including figures from Kry, 20VC, Voi, and Synthesia—have formally warned that the Act's compliance burdens are suffocating domestic innovation and demanded a pause on its implementation.

The friction centers heavily on the General-Purpose AI (GPAI) code of practice. While technically a "voluntary checklist" designed to help organizations interpret the law, it retains elements that the industry views as overly prescriptive, such as mandatory external evaluations and stringent data transparency requirements. Unlike U.S. hyperscalers that view regulatory compliance as a mere operating expense, European startups lack the capital to absorb these costs.

Consequently, the AI Act currently acts as a severe handicap. As one startup founder noted at a recent industry summit, the assumption that compliance travels seamlessly across borders is a misconception. European companies attempting to scale globally find themselves bogged down by unique regional demands, diverting scarce venture capital toward legal overhead rather than productive compute.

### The Brussels Mirage: De Jure vs. De Facto Diffusion

Legal scholars have increasingly questioned the efficacy of the Brussels Effect in the context of AI. A comprehensive study published in the _LSE Law Review_ highlights what it terms the "Brussels Mirage". The study draws a critical distinction between _de jure_ diffusion (the formal legal application of EU law extraterritorially) and _de facto_ diffusion (market-driven regulatory alignment).

While _de facto_ diffusion works well for consumer products, it faces severe structural limitations in high-risk, state-controlled domains such as predictive policing. Because the EU AI Act was grounded in Article 114 TFEU (the internal market legal basis), it functions structurally as a "product-safety instrument" rather than a fundamental rights charter. What gets exported through the Brussels Effect is merely the _compliance form_—technical documentation, conformity assessments, and impact assessment procedures.

The LSE study illustrates this by examining the deployment of AI by police forces in India (e.g., the Trinetra and CMAPS systems). In jurisdictions without a strong culture of ex-ante accountability, the Brussels Effect becomes distorted. AI vendors can supply high-risk tools to state governments accompanied by EU-style technical documentation. The deploying state authorities then point to this documentation as evidence of due diligence. However, the state conducts no independent Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment and imposes no genuine human oversight. Consequently, the compliance paperwork travels, but the rights-protective substance is completely left behind. Instead of constraining state power, the _de facto_ European standard inadvertently legitimizes high-risk deployments by authoritarian or illiberal actors.

### The Pursuit of Sovereign AI and its Traps

Recognizing its lag in foundational innovation, Europe has aggressively pivoted toward the concept of "Sovereign AI." Sovereign AI is the strategic imperative to build domestic AI technology, informed by homegrown data, and powered by localized supercomputers or "AI factories". The rationale is multifold: protecting specific cultural identities, securing sensitive government data from foreign extraction, and boosting domestic research competitiveness.

Initiatives are proliferating rapidly across the bloc. Denmark recently unveiled a sovereign AI supercomputer, notably funded by the proceeds of highly popular Danish weight-loss drugs like Ozempic, aimed at supporting biotech and pharmaceutical research. Italy has deployed an AI factory anchored by a supercomputer to evolve a dedicated language model tailored specifically for government workers. Sweden, attempting to rectify its 25th-place ranking in the Global AI Index, is completely revamping its national supercomputer to retain and recruit premier global AI talent. For nations like France and the UK, where public trust in AI is exceptionally low (only one in three people trust AI), sovereign models serve as a political necessity to assure citizens that their data is not being culturally overwritten by Silicon Valley algorithms.

However, the pursuit of Sovereign AI is fraught with what experts term "sovereignty traps". Training state-of-the-art large language models requires capital expenditures often exceeding $1 billion—an astronomical hardware, energy, and staffing cost that fragmented European states struggle to meet independently. Geopolitical friction regarding access to advanced microchips further warps the playing field. Additionally, by retreating into national silos to train AI purely on local sets of values, European countries risk undermining cross-border scientific collaboration and duplicating efforts, ultimately preventing the continent from scaling a cohesive competitor to the U.S. or China.

## Part 5: Synthesis: The Divergent Optima of the Global AI Value Stack

The empirical evidence definitively demonstrates that the AI value stack does not possess a single global shape. The optimum shape of the value stack is determined exogenously by the geopolitical, capital, and regulatory environments of the host bloc.

### The Top-Heavy Optimum (United States)

In the United States, the optimal value stack is top-heavy and vertically integrated. Value lands predominantly at the hardware layer (Nvidia) and the foundation model/cloud infrastructure layer (Microsoft, Google, AWS, OpenAI, Anthropic). The economic surplus is generated by establishing a quasi-monopoly on frontier capabilities and charging access rents via APIs. The strategic moat is sheer scale—a capital and compute barrier that cannot be crossed by smaller actors. This ecosystem thrives on innovation but risks structural fragility due to a lack of coordinated diffusion strategy into the broader industrial base.

### The Bottom-Heavy Optimum (China)

In China, the optimal value stack is bottom-heavy and horizontally commoditized. Because China cannot currently access the frontier hardware required to out-scale the U.S. due to export embargoes, its strategic optimum is to destroy the economic value of the foundation model layer entirely by open-sourcing it (e.g., DeepSeek, 01.AI). By turning the model into a free, highly optimized commodity, China forces the economic value to land downstream—at the application, system integration, and enterprise deployment layers. Value is extracted through rapid diffusion into traditional industries (manufacturing, supply chain, energy) and by establishing Chinese architectures as the foundational infrastructure for the Global South.

### The Regulatory Optimum (Europe)

In Europe, the optimal value stack is regulatory-centric and localized. The European optimum attempts to capture value at the governance and physical infrastructure layer. Lacking dominance in both frontier models and widespread commercial application, Europe seeks to mandate itself as the indispensable arbiter of AI trust. Value here lands in compliance architecture, auditing ecosystems, and localized Sovereign AI hardware. While the AI Act currently acts as a severe handicap that stifles dynamic startup growth and drains venture capital into legal compliance, this optimum bets on a future where multinational enterprises and governments will eventually pay a massive premium for sovereign, highly regulated, and legally insulated AI environments.

Ultimately, the redistribution of economic surplus in the AI era will not be uniform. Value will land where geopolitical blocs construct the most effective gravitational pull. Understanding these divergent optima is paramount for recognizing how the future of global economic productivity will be shaped not merely by the algorithms themselves, but by the geopolitical constraints engineered around them.
