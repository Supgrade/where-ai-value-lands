# Capital-Labor Divergence under AI

> [!abstract] One-line
> Even granting modest AI productivity gains, the structural mechanism appears to **direct national income toward capital owners** (firm equity, IP holders, GPU/datacenter capital) **away from labor** — with specific demographic groups bearing the cost.

## The mechanism
1. AI automates a profitable subset of tasks (see [[task-based-framework]]: ~4.6% of US tasks).
2. The tasks AI most helps are concentrated at the **lower-skill end** — entry-level work, summarization, routing, copy.
3. Productivity boost for low-skill workers makes them **more replaceable**, not more valuable.
4. Displaced labor competes for the **shrinking pool of non-automated tasks** → real-wage pressure across the lower distribution.
5. Without a deliberate research shift toward **human-complementary tasks** (new roles that AI helps humans do, rather than tasks AI takes from humans), the trend is structural, not cyclical.

## Empirical pattern so far (2024–2025)
No apocalyptic mass displacement yet. What is visible:
- **Task reallocation** within roles rather than wholesale role elimination.
- **Entry-level job saturation** — fewer slots, more applicants, longer ramps.
- **Within-firm role consolidation** — one human plus AI scaffolding doing what previously required several.

Wage effects are early and noisy, but theoretical models predict steady downward pressure on the lower half of the distribution without policy intervention.

## Demographic specificity (per [[acemoglu-simple-macroeconomics]])
- AI-exposed tasks are more **demographically even** than prior automation waves (which concentrated on male blue-collar manufacturing).
- However, the **wage incidence is uneven**: empirical projections point to a specific negative effect on **low-education, native-born women** in administrative and routine cognitive work.
- No projection that AI **reduces** overall labor income inequality. Multiple projections show it **widens** it.

## The capital side
The other side of the coin:
- National income increasingly flows to **firm owners, IP holders, GPU/datacenter capital, and frontier-lab equity**.
- If [[circular-ai-economy]] is correct, much of this "income" is currently VC-recycled and unstable. If it is wrong, the divergence is durable and consequential.

## GDP-vs-welfare wedge
A subtle but important corollary: AI lowers the cost of producing tasks with **negative social value** — algorithmic manipulation, SEO spam, hyper-targeted exploitative advertising, deepfakes. These activities **inflate GDP** through ad spend and digital traffic while **reducing welfare**. The headline number ("AI added X% to GDP") may be true and misleading simultaneously.

## Why this matters for *Where Value Lands*
- Sharpens the "redistribution of surplus" framing in [[00_initial-brief]]: the question is not just *where* value lands across the stack, but *whether it lands on labor at all*.
- Adds an ethical and political dimension to scenarios in [[03_structure]] that the paper's audience (capital-rich operators) will care about for both reputational and macro-stability reasons.
- Suggests an **operator move** worth flagging in the practical half: investing in human-complementary scaffolding (training data for new roles, evals that reward AI-human composition, regulated-vertical workflows where humans must remain in the loop).

## Why this might be wrong
- Same general critique as [[task-based-framework]]: framework assumes a fixed output structure. Genuinely new human-complementary work *might* emerge endogenously — it has in past technology transitions, though typically with painful lags.
- Demographic projections rely on stable occupational data; AI may reshape the occupational map fast enough to invalidate the baselines.
- Political/policy interventions (re-skilling, tax structure, universal compute access) could change the incidence materially.
