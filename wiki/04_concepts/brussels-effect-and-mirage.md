# The Brussels Effect and the Brussels Mirage

> [!abstract] Idea
> The **Brussels Effect** is the phenomenon whereby the EU leverages its single-market scale to dictate global corporate behavior — non-EU firms standardize their global operations to EU compliance levels rather than managing fragmented regulatory regimes. The EU's bet is that the AI Act will repeat this for AI. The **Brussels Mirage**, articulated in a 2026 *LSE Law Review* analysis, identifies the structural reason this bet is failing in AI: only the *form* of compliance travels abroad, not the *substance* of rights protection.

## The Effect (as it worked for GDPR / DMA / DSA)

- EU sets a stringent standard backed by access to ~450M consumers.
- Multinationals comply globally because regional differentiation is operationally more expensive than universal EU-grade compliance.
- The EU therefore exports norms without exporting jurisdiction — *regulatory imperialism* by market pull. This is the third of the three "digital empires" exports ([[digital-empires-tripartite]]).

## The Mirage (as it is now failing for AI)

The LSE *Brussels Mirage* study draws a critical distinction:

- **De jure diffusion** — formal extraterritorial application of EU law.
- **De facto diffusion** — market-driven regulatory alignment.

De facto diffusion works well for consumer products (privacy, content moderation). It fails for **high-risk, state-controlled domains** like predictive policing.

**Why.** The EU AI Act is grounded in Article 114 TFEU — the internal market legal basis. It functions structurally as a *product-safety instrument*, not a fundamental-rights charter. What gets exported is therefore the compliance *form*: technical documentation, conformity assessments, impact-assessment procedures. What does **not** get exported is the rights-protective *substance*.

**The Indian case study.** Trinetra and CMAPS predictive-policing systems in India are deployed with EU-style technical documentation attached. State authorities point to this paperwork as evidence of due diligence — without conducting an independent Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment and without genuine human oversight. The compliance form *legitimizes* the deployment rather than constraining it. Instead of exporting fundamental rights, the EU has inadvertently exported a *cover story* that authoritarian or illiberal actors can use to wrap high-risk AI deployments in respectability.

## Why this matters for value capture

If the Brussels Effect works for AI, the EU captures real value at the compliance-licensing layer — global enterprises pay an EU premium for trusted, auditable AI environments ([[divergent-value-stack-optima]] — regulatory-centric optimum).

If the Brussels Mirage is the truer picture, the EU's regulatory bet exports paperwork without exporting power. Value capture collapses; the AI Act becomes purely a domestic compliance burden ([[sovereign-ai]] becomes the only remaining lever).

## The handicap-or-moat question, sharpened

- **Short-to-medium term: handicap.** Direct market evidence — the July 2025 open letter from 50+ European founders and VCs (Kry, 20VC, Voi, Synthesia) demanding implementation pause — confirms the AI Act is draining scarce VC capital toward legal overhead. The General-Purpose AI (GPAI) code of practice is cited specifically: mandatory external evaluations and stringent data-transparency requirements that US hyperscalers absorb as opex but EU startups cannot.
- **Long term: potentially a moat, but conditional.** Only if (a) global enterprise/government AI demand matures around verifiable safety + auditability, *and* (b) the Brussels Mirage thesis is wrong, *and* (c) the EU domestic ecosystem survives the short-term capital drain. All three are contestable.
