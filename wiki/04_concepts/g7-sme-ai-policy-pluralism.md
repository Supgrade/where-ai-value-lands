# G7 SME AI policy pluralism

The OECD G7 discussion paper's country profiles reveal **systematic variation** in how the seven advanced economies are trying to push AI into their SME bases. The variation is interesting because it doesn't map cleanly onto the existing [[digital-empires-tripartite]] frame (US market / China state / EU rights). It cuts orthogonally in some places and confirms the frame in others.

## The four observable models

1. **Market-led + thin federal SME programmes (US).** Federal role limited to enabling infrastructure (NAIRR), standards (NIST), seed funds (SBIR/STTR), and an SBA AI Resource Hub. Most capital flows through private VC. The 2025 AI Action Plan calls for *reducing* regulatory burden. Workforce policy: extend tax code to qualify AI literacy as educational assistance.
2. **State-coordinated industrial policy + sovereignty (France, Italy, partially Germany).** Heavy use of national strategies tied to industrial policy. Big-ticket sovereign-compute investments (France EUR 109bn AI pledge, Italian IT4LIA AI Factory, German Jupiter AI Factory). Subsidy-driven SME support (IA Booster, Transition 5.0, KI4KMU). EuroHPC AI Factory network as the binding multilateral layer.
3. **State-coordinated + soft-law (Japan, UK).** Strong central direction (Japan's AI Act, UK's AI Opportunities Action Plan + Modern Industrial Strategy) but with light regulation. Japan: large capex commitments (USD 65bn), Rapidus semiconductor consortium, soft-law sectoral guidance, catalogue subsidies. UK: GBP 2bn Compute Roadmap, AI Growth Zones, AIRR expansion, sector-specific BridgeAI.
4. **Regionally-delivered federal (Canada).** Federal funding + regional delivery via RDAs. CAD 2.4bn over five years; AI Compute Access Fund (CAD 300m); CIFAR-coordinated Pan-Canadian AI Strategy. Strong emphasis on technological sovereignty and SME compute access.

## Does this confirm or complicate the tripartite frame?

**Partially confirms.** The US really is the most market-driven; the EU members (France, Germany, Italy) really do gravitate toward state-coordinated, rights-respecting frameworks; the broad regulatory posture matches [[brussels-effect-and-mirage]].

**But it complicates in three ways:**

1. **Japan as a fourth model.** Japan combines state coordination with soft law and turnkey subsidies. It is neither market-led nor rights-led nor state-controlled in the China sense. The Hiroshima AI Process (HAIP) — a Japanese G7-presidency initiative that produced the *International Code of Conduct for Organisations Developing Advanced AI Systems* — positions Japan as a normative leader without EU-style hard regulation. This is a genuine fourth axis. See [[divergent-value-stack-optima]].
2. **The UK is its own thing.** Post-Brexit it has explicitly chosen "state-led + market-enabled" rather than Brussels-style horizontal regulation. The Modern Industrial Strategy + AI Action Plan combination looks more Japanese than European.
3. **Canada bridges US and EU.** Federally funded, regionally delivered, sovereignty-tinged but private-sector-anchored. The "Canadian Sovereign AI Compute Strategy" explicitly uses sovereignty language — but executes through partnerships with private compute.

## Common ground

Despite different rhetorics, **G7 instruments converge on a narrow set of levers**:

- Compute access (Canada AI Compute Access Fund; UK AIRR; US NAIRR; Germany Jupiter; France AI2F; Italy IT4LIA; Japan ¥500bn subsidy programmes)
- Sectoral SME subsidies (Italy Transition 5.0; France IA Booster; Japan Catalog-Type; UK BridgeAI; Germany KI4KMU)
- Skills/training programmes (UK Flexible AI Upskilling Fund; France Osez l'IA AI Academy; Germany AI Studios; Japan METI guidebooks; US SBA AI Resource Hub)
- Innovation hubs / regional delivery (Italy Competence Centres; Germany Mittelstand-Digital; France IA-Clusters; Canada RDAs; US Small Business Development Centers)

The **convergence on instruments alongside divergence in framing** is a finding in its own right: G7 governments quote different ideologies but reach for the same toolkit when actually pushing AI into SMEs.

## The barrier-and-support survey

Table 4 in the OECD survey adds another layer of pluralism — different SMEs report different *perceived* barriers and preferred supports:

| Country | Top reported barrier | Top requested support |
|---|---|---|
| Canada | Generative AI not suited to our work | Training |
| Germany | Clients would not approve | Information campaigns |
| Japan | Employees lack skills | Financial assistance |
| UK | Generative AI not suited to our work | Training |

Germany's response is the most striking: client/customer disapproval of AI is the leading barrier — a **trust- and reputation-driven** constraint, not a skills or money one. This is uniquely European in flavour and provides empirical anchor for the rights/trust posture documented in [[bradford-digital-empires]].

## Implications for the working hypotheses

- **[[divergent-value-stack-optima]]** sharpens: at the SME-policy layer, four optima exist (not three), with Japan and the UK as distinct.
- **[[digital-empires-tripartite]]** partially holds at the level of rhetoric and regulatory framework, less so at the level of operational instruments — these converge.
- **[[diffusion-vs-innovation]]**: every G7 country has now publicly committed to diffusion as a strategic objective, regardless of bloc. That is *new* relative to a 2020 baseline.
- **[[H1_L0-L7-ladder]]**: nation-states differ less on *what* layer they target than on *how* they finance access to it.
