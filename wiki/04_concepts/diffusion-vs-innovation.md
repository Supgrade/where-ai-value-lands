# Innovation vs. Diffusion — The GPT Frame

> [!abstract] Idea
> Per Jeffrey Ding ([[ding-diffusion-marathon]]), historical economic power transitions driven by General Purpose Technologies (GPTs) are **rarely decided by the initial invention**. They are decided by **diffusion capacity** — the ability of a nation to spread and adopt the technology across all productive sectors. The current US framing of the AI race as an AGI *sprint* is therefore strategically misframed.

## Historical analogs

- **Second Industrial Revolution.** The US overtook Britain not by inventing electricity or the internal combustion engine but by *diffusing* them rapidly across a vast industrial base.
- **Postwar USSR.** Massive innovation capacity (first satellite, major physics breakthroughs) but a fatal **diffusion deficit** — scientific triumphs never reached broad economic productivity.

The implication: the GPT winner is whoever absorbs the technology into the macroeconomy fastest, not whoever invents the most advanced version first.

## Application to the current US/China divide

**United States.** Strong absolute innovation, strong relative diffusion capability (deep engineering base, capable absorptive institutions). But policy is over-indexed on the "leading-sector mechanism" — frontier breakthroughs — and on restricting foreign access. This neglects the domestic diffusion mandate. Outcome risk: a top-heavy economy where AI surplus is trapped inside the tech sector itself.

**China.** Strong innovation capacity (patents, publications) but historically a **diffusion deficit** caused by uneven STEM education and institutional bottlenecks that isolated breakthroughs in elite institutions. The strategic pivot — described in [[open-weight-asymmetry]] and [[lee-01ai-pivot]] — is a deliberate attempt to close this gap by forcing AI downstream into traditional industries (banking, insurance, mining, energy).

**OECD measurement layer.** [[oecd-sme-ai-adoption-2025]] supplies the first systematic G7-wide measurement of diffusion at the SME tail — the literal bottom of the diffusion S-curve. Findings: OECD-wide AI adoption is at 14% in 2024 (firms 10+ employees), with **40% large vs 11.9% small** firms adopting and only **29% of generative-AI-using SMEs** deploying it in core business activities. **Construction, hospitality, transport, retail** — the bulk of SME-heavy sectors — sit at 7–12% adoption vs ~45% in ICT (see [[sectoral-ai-diffusion-pattern]]). G7 core-business AI adoption ranges from 1.9% (Japan) to 6.1% (US). These numbers make Ding's diffusion-marathon thesis empirically operational: the diffusion gap is not a theoretical risk but a measurable artifact, and it is widening in absolute terms (sectoral leaders pulled away from laggards by 15 pp in 3 years).

## Why this is the key economic concept

The whole "where does value land" question depends on whether AI behaves as a GPT or not. If it does:
- The current AGI-sprint framing is the wrong mental model.
- The value will land wherever a bloc successfully diffuses the technology *across* sectors, not where it pioneers the next model.
- This pushes the long-run answer toward the application layer, the B2B enterprise integration layer, and the SME absorptive layer — i.e., away from the bottom of the US-shaped U.

Combined with [[task-based-framework]] (which caps US TFP gains from AI at <0.71% cumulative if the diffusion does *not* happen), this is one of the most important economic concepts in the wiki.

## Supporting evidence

The OECD G7 discussion paper on SME AI adoption (2025) operationalises this thesis at the firm level. Its entire policy half — the [[oecd-sme-enabler-quartet]] (connectivity, AI-enabling inputs, skills, finance), the [[sme-policy-pathway-novice-to-champion]] matrix, and the [[g7-sme-ai-policy-pluralism]] of instruments — is a diffusion playbook applied to SMEs. The paper notes that nearly 70 countries have adopted national AI strategies; the convergence of G7 instruments on compute-access, sectoral subsidies, skills programmes and innovation hubs (despite divergent rhetorics) is itself evidence that diffusion has become a recognised policy frontier alongside frontier-model invention.

## Tensions

- "Diffusion capacity" is hard to measure directly. The historical analogs are post-hoc; whether anyone can identify a diffusion bottleneck *in real time* is unclear.
- The US may diffuse AI faster than this critique assumes. Coding agents, copilots, and consumer chat have penetrated end-user workflows at speed.
- China's "diffusion pivot" is recent. Whether 01.AI's B2B traction in banking/insurance/mining generalizes — or whether it remains scattered industry pilots — is an open question.
- **The US/China asymmetry inside the OECD data.** OECD ICT survey shows the US at unusually *low* aggregate adoption (3–6%) but with a strong young-firm / start-up adoption signal. This is consistent with Ding's claim that the US over-indexes on the leading-sector mechanism (frontier startups) and under-invests in broad SME diffusion. China is not in the OECD survey frame, but the [[lee-01ai-pivot]] B2B-diffusion strategy is a deliberate response to a measurement Ding implied — China is operationalizing a diffusion-first agenda from the opposite direction.
- **Binding constraints made concrete.** The OECD evidence sharpens one mechanism: if AI diffusion is governed by SME-level absorptive capacity, then the binding constraint is human capital (see [[ai-skill-shortage-as-diffusion-bottleneck]]) and to a lesser extent connectivity (see [[sme-connectivity-divide]]) and finance (see [[sme-ai-finance-gap]]). The Ding-style thesis becomes empirically testable at this level: does a country's diffusion capacity correlate more with its training and broadband stack than with its frontier-model investment? Italy (4.7× large-vs-small AI-adoption ratio) and the US (1.65×) bracket the spread within the G7.
