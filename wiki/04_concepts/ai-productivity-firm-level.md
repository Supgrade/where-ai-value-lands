# AI–Productivity Link at the Firm Level

> AI adopters are more productive than non-adopters. **But most of the premium is not caused by AI** — it reflects pre-existing competitiveness, digital readiness, and workforce capabilities. Once these are controlled for, the AI-specific productivity premium shrinks substantially.

This is the empirical *and* methodological caveat at the heart of the AI-productivity literature, as synthesized by [[oecd-sme-ai-adoption-2025]] (Calvino & Fontanelli 2023; Calvino, Costa & Haerle, forthcoming; Acemoglu et al. 2022; McElheran et al. 2024).

## The headline empirical findings

- **Productivity-quantile gap.** Share of AI adopters in the top decile of the productivity distribution vs. the bottom decile: **+40% in France (2018)**, **+120% in Germany (2018)**, **+240% in Italy (2020)**. Adopters are concentrated at the top.
- **Within-cohort premium.** Comparing firms of the same size, age, and sector, AI users have labour-productivity premia of **>4% in most G7 countries, sometimes >15%**. Documented in Canada, France, Germany, Italy, UK, US.
- **Macro projection.** [[oecd-g7-productivity-gain-projection]] — annual labour-productivity gains from AI of **0.2 to 1.3 percentage points** across G7 economies over the next decade, depending on the adoption scenario. For reference: the US ICT boom of the mid-1990s delivered 0.5–1.5 pp.

## The methodological caveat

The headline gap is not all causal. Three confounders consistently shrink the estimated AI premium when added:

1. **Pre-existing competitiveness.** Firms that are *already* more digital and more competitive are *also* more likely to adopt AI. The productivity gap partly reflects selection, not treatment.
2. **Digital capabilities.** Once cloud computing, other digital technologies, and ICT-specialist presence are controlled for, the AI premium shrinks substantially (Acemoglu et al. 2022; Calvino & Fontanelli 2023).
3. **Worker skills.** Firms with more ICT-trained workers benefit more from AI — the premium is mediated by workforce capability, not by AI per se.

The honest interpretation: **AI is a multiplier of pre-existing digital and human capital**, not an independent productivity-boost driver. This is one of the most important findings in the source for our paper.

## The J-shaped productivity dynamic

McElheran et al. (2024) document a **J-shaped** firm-level productivity trajectory after AI adoption in US manufacturing: productivity often **declines before rising**, consistent with Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson's (2021) "complementary-investments-take-time" model of general-purpose technologies.

This matters because:

- **Macro projections that compound today's adopter-premium forward will overstate the gain.** Some current adopters are still in the J's downward leg; some non-adopters will accelerate once they cross the J's pivot.
- **Returns to AI are gated by organizational redesign.** Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb (2023) argue the AI surplus is unlocked by reorganization, not by the technology in isolation. Firms that adopt AI without restructuring workflows capture little.

## Why this is load-bearing for the U-curve

- **It re-routes surplus.** If AI's productivity gains are mediated by *complementary* digital capital (cloud, data, skills), then the surplus does not stop at AI vendors — it accrues to whoever built and owns the complementary stack. For most SMEs, that complementary stack is **rented from hyperscalers and SaaS vendors**. The application-layer firm captures the *operational* productivity gain; the substrate vendor captures the *recurring rent*. Cross-check [[H2_u-curve-of-value]] — the bottom of the U is reinforced by exactly this mediation.
- **It explains the [[scaling-gap]].** If AI productivity gains depend on complementary investments and organizational redesign, the 74% of firms struggling to scale AI are not necessarily failing at AI — they are failing at the *complements*. The lever is enablers, not models.
- **It complicates the SME productivity story.** SMEs have less of the complementary stack (cloud, ICT specialists, training budgets). Their AI premium is therefore likely to be *smaller* than the headline cross-firm regression suggests — both because they have less complementary capital and because they tend to use AI for peripheral tasks (only 29% in core activities, per [[oecd-sme-ai-adoption-2025]]).

## Generative AI as a special case

The OECD source argues generative AI exhibits the canonical general-purpose-technology markers:

- **Pervasiveness** — usable across sectors and functions.
- **Continuous improvement** — model capability rising on a steep slope.
- **Innovation-spawning** — accelerating R&D, lowering entrepreneurship entry barriers.

In SME surveys (OECD 2025), generative AI shows up as the most-used AI type, and **39% of SMEs that adopted generative AI and had a recent skill gap reported it helped compensate**. This is a different kind of productivity gain: not "AI does the task faster" but "AI substitutes for missing skills" — a substitutability story that could be either bullish (broadening the productive workforce) or bearish (compressing skilled-labour wage premia). See [[capital-labor-divergence]].

## Tensions

- **Causal identification is hard.** All the cited studies are observational. Selection bias is partially controlled but not fully eliminated.
- **The premium varies by AI type.** Firms developing AI internally (Calvino & Fontanelli 2024 for France) capture larger premia than firms buying externally — a finding that pushes value upstream toward Makers / Shapers and away from Takers, conditional on firms having the capital to develop in-house. Most SMEs cannot.
- **Macro and micro estimates disagree in scale.** The +15% within-cohort labour-productivity premium does not aggregate cleanly to the 0.2–1.3 pp annual macro projection — too many firms have not adopted, and the J-curve drag is unmodelled.
- **[[task-based-framework]] is the bear cap.** Acemoglu's task-based estimate suggests cumulative US TFP gains from AI of **<0.71% over a decade** — incompatible with the upper end of the OECD projection. The two views are unreconciled.
